The Induction Decision in TBI

An Evidence-Based Comparison of Ketamine and Propofol for Rapid Sequence Induction (RSI) in UK Emergency Departments

The Critical Choice

In patients with Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI), Rapid Sequence Induction (RSI) for intubation is a high-stakes procedure. The choice of induction agent can profoundly impact patient outcomes by influencing cerebral haemodynamics. Historically, ketamine was avoided due to fears of raising intracranial pressure (ICP). This infographic synthesizes modern UK-centric evidence to compare ketamine and propofol, guiding clinicians towards a data-driven decision that prioritizes patient safety and neurological protection by avoiding secondary brain insults.

Agent Profiles: A Head-to-Head Comparison

Ketamine: The Sympathomimetic

Known for its haemodynamic stability, ketamine supports blood pressure, a critical factor in maintaining cerebral perfusion.

  • ✓
    Haemodynamics: Stable or increased Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP), preserving Cerebral Perfusion Pressure (CPP).
  • ✓
    ICP: Modern evidence shows no significant increase in ICP in mechanically ventilated TBI patients.
  • ✓
    Neuroprotection: Potential benefit via NMDA receptor antagonism, mitigating excitotoxicity.

Propofol: The Cerebral Vasoconstrictor

Valued for its ability to reduce cerebral metabolism, but this comes at the cost of potential cardiovascular instability.

  • ✗
    Haemodynamics: High risk of significant hypotension due to vasodilation, which can critically lower CPP.
  • ✓
    ICP: Effectively lowers ICP by reducing cerebral metabolic rate (CMRO2).
  • ✓
    Neuroprotection: Achieved by reducing the brain's metabolic demand.

The Primary Differentiator: Haemodynamic Impact

The most significant risk in TBI induction is hypotension, which leads to secondary brain injury. This chart compares the typical post-induction change in Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP), a key factor in maintaining Cerebral Perfusion Pressure (CPP = MAP - ICP).

This bar chart illustrates that propofol carries a substantial risk of hypotension, whereas ketamine typically maintains or elevates blood pressure, safeguarding cerebral perfusion.

Debunking the ICP Myth

1970s

Initial studies in patients with pre-existing CSF circulation issues suggested ketamine raised ICP.

2000s

Research in modern settings with controlled ventilation began to challenge the old dogma, showing neutral ICP effects.

2020s

Systematic reviews confirm ketamine does not increase ICP in ventilated TBI patients compared to other agents.

The long-held belief that ketamine is contraindicated in TBI is based on outdated evidence. Modern research confirms its safety regarding intracranial pressure when used in a controlled RSI setting. The focus has now shifted to its significant benefit of preserving cerebral perfusion.

The Bottom Line: A Shift in Practice

Based on the current body of evidence, **ketamine is the preferred induction agent for the majority of patients with traumatic brain injury in the UK.** Its superior haemodynamic profile provides a crucial safety margin by preventing hypotension and preserving cerebral perfusion pressure. While propofol has a role in reducing ICP, this benefit is often negated by its cardiovascular effects. The paradigm has shifted: the primary goal is to "Protect the Penumbra" by ensuring stable cerebral perfusion, a goal more reliably achieved with ketamine.
